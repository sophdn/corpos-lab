[INST] The tides of the ocean arise from the gravitational attraction between the Earth, the Moon, and the Sun. Every mass in the solar system pulls on every other mass, and the strength of that pull falls off with the square of the distance between them. Because the ocean is a fluid layer spread across a rotating planet, it responds to these pulls by shifting its shape over the course of each day. The Moon, though far smaller than the Sun, sits close enough to the Earth that its gravitational influence on the ocean is roughly twice as strong as the Sun's. For this reason the lunar rhythm dominates the pattern of the tides across most of the world's coastlines. The essential cause of a tide is not the raw pull of the Moon but the difference in that pull across the width of the Earth. The side of the planet nearest the Moon feels a slightly stronger attraction than the planet's center, while the far side feels a slightly weaker one. This gradient stretches the ocean into two bulges, one facing the Moon and one directly opposite it. As the Earth rotates beneath these bulges, a given stretch of coastline passes through regions of raised and lowered water. Most shorelines therefore experience two high waters and two low waters in a little over a day, a rhythm known as the semidiurnal pattern. The Sun adds a second, weaker cycle layered on top of the lunar one. When the Sun, the Earth, and the Moon fall into a line, near the times of the new moon and the full moon, the solar pull reinforces the lunar pull. The two bulges grow, and the difference between high and low water reaches its widest. Mariners call these the spring tides, a name that has nothing to do with the season and everything to do with the water springing upward. When the Sun and the Moon stand at right angles to each other, near the quarter phases, their pulls partly cancel. The difference between high and low water then shrinks to its smallest, and these are the neap tides. The vertical distance between high water and low water is called the tidal range, and it varies enormously from place to place. In the open ocean, far from any coast, the range may be little more than half a meter. Near certain coastlines the range grows to many meters.
