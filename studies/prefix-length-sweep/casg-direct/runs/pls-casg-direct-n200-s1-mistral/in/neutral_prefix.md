The tides of the ocean arise from the gravitational attraction between the Earth, the Moon, and the Sun. Every mass in the solar system pulls on every other mass, and the strength of that pull falls off with the square of the distance between them. Because the ocean is a fluid layer spread across a rotating planet, it responds to these pulls by shifting its shape over the course of each day. The Moon, though far smaller than the Sun, sits close enough to the Earth that its gravitational influence on the ocean is roughly twice as strong as the Sun's. For this reason the lunar rhythm dominates the pattern of the tides across most of the world's coastlines. The essential cause of a tide is not the raw pull of the Moon but the difference in that pull across the width of the Earth. The side of the planet nearest the Moon feels a slightly stronger attraction than the planet's center, while the far side feels a slightly weaker one. This gradient stretches the ocean into two bulges, one facing the Moon and one directly opposite it. As the Earth rotates beneath these bulges, a given stretch of coastline passes through regions of raised and lowered water.
