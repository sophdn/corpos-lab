The tides are caused by the combined gravitational forces of the Earth, Moon, and Sun. The Moon's influence is stronger due to its proximity to the Earth, causing two high waters and two low waters every day in a pattern known as the semidiurnal pattern. The Sun's influence adds a weaker cycle on top of the lunar one.

During new moon and full moon, when the Sun, Earth, and Moon align, the solar pull reinforces the lunar pull, causing larger tidal ranges known as spring tides. Conversely, during quarter phases, the pulls partly cancel, resulting in smaller tidal ranges known as neap tides.

The vertical distance between high water and low water is called the tidal range, which varies greatly from place to place. The Bay of Fundy in North America records some of the largest tidal ranges due to its shape and natural sloshing period.
