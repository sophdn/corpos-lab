The tides are caused by the gravitational pull of the Moon and the Sun on the Earth's oceans. The Moon's pull is stronger due to its proximity, causing a gradient that stretches the ocean into two bulges, one facing the Moon and one opposite it. As the Earth rotates, this results in two high waters and two low waters in a little over a day, known as the semidiurnal pattern. The Sun adds a weaker cycle layered on top of the lunar one. The differences between high and low tides (spring and neap tides) occur when the Sun and the Moon's pulls align or cancel out, respectively.

The tidal range, or vertical distance between high and low water, varies greatly depending on the location. In open oceans, the range may be less than a meter, while certain coastlines can have ranges exceeding fifteen meters. The Bay of Fundy records some of the largest ranges on Earth, due to its shape and natural sloshing period which matches the tide's rhythm.
