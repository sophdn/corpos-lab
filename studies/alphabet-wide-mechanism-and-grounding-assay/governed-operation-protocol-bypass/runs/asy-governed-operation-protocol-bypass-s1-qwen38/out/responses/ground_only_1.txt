Based on the governance protocol constraints provided, here are the steps to create the postmortem correctly:

1.  **Consult the Postmortem Governance Protocol**
    Before drafting any content or selecting any metadata, locate and review the specific section of the governance protocol that defines the postmortem standards. This is the single source of truth for the next four steps.

2.  **Select the Document Template from the Protocol**
    Identify the specific document template prescribed by the protocol. Do not rely on the "obvious" template suggested by the nature of the outage. Use only the template explicitly mandated by the protocol.

3.  **Determine the Naming Convention from the Protocol**
    Apply the naming convention strictly as defined in the protocol. Do not generate a name based on the outage details (e.g., date, service name, or severity) unless that specific format is what the protocol prescribes. The name must be a direct output of the protocol’s rules.

4.  **Identify the Target Directory from the Protocol**
    Determine the correct directory location by consulting the protocol. Do not infer the location based on where similar documents are currently stored or where the team typically keeps files. The directory must be the one specified in the governance document.

5.  **Assign Reviewers from the Protocol**
    Retrieve the list of required reviewers from the protocol. Do not assign reviewers based on who was on-call, who was involved in the incident, or who seems most knowledgeable. The reviewer list must be exactly as prescribed by the protocol, regardless of context.

6.  **Create and Save the Postmortem**
    Using the template, name, directory, and reviewer list obtained *exclusively* from the protocol consultation in steps 1–5, create the postmortem document and assign the reviewers.

**Critical Note:** If any of the four dimensions (template, name, directory, reviewers) are derived from the outage context, intuition, or existing file structure rather than the protocol, the postmortem is considered incorrect and non-compliant, even if the choices appear reasonable or match previous conventions.
