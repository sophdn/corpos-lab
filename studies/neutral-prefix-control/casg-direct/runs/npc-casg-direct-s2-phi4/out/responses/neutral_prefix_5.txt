The ocean tides are primarily caused by the gravitational interaction between the Earth, the Moon, and the Sun. Despite the Sun being much larger than the Moon, the Moon's proximity to Earth results in a stronger gravitational influence on the ocean, about twice as strong as that of the Sun. This gravitational pull varies across the Earth's surface, creating a gradient that stretches the ocean into two bulges: one on the side facing the Moon and another on the opposite side. As the Earth rotates, these bulges lead to the regular rise and fall of sea levels, producing the semidiurnal tide pattern, which involves two high tides and two low tides each day for most coastlines.

The Sun also contributes to tidal patterns, albeit more weakly. When the Sun, Earth, and Moon align (during new and full moons), their gravitational forces combine to create spring tides, characterized by the largest difference between high and low tides. Conversely, when the Sun and Moon form a right angle relative to Earth (during quarter moons), the forces partially cancel each other out, resulting in neap tides, which have the smallest tidal range.

The tidal range, or the vertical difference between high and low tides, varies greatly depending on geographic and oceanographic factors. In open oceans, the range is minimal, but it can be significantly larger near certain coastlines due to factors like the shape of the basin and resonance effects. For example, the Bay of Fundy on the eastern coast of North America experiences some of the world's highest tidal ranges, exceeding fifteen meters during spring tides, largely because the basin's natural sloshing period aligns closely with the tidal period.

Tidal patterns are not uniform globally; some regions experience diurnal tides with a single high and low tide each day, while others have mixed tides with two high or low waters of differing heights. These variations are influenced by the tilt of the Moon's orbit and the specific geography of each ocean basin, which affects how tidal waves are channeled and reflected.

The mathematical modeling of tides involves breaking them down into simple oscillations with periods derived from the movements of the Moon and the Sun. Coastal authorities use these models to forecast tidal heights far into the future. Accompanying the vertical movement of tides is the horizontal movement of water known as tidal currents, which can significantly impact marine ecology, especially in estuaries and mudflats, by transporting sediment, nutrients, and marine larvae.
