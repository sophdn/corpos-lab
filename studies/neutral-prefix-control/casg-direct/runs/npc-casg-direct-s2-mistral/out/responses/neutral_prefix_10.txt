 In this text, the explanation of ocean tides and their causes, patterns, and variations is detailed. The primary forces driving tides are the gravitational attractions between the Earth, Moon, and Sun. The Moon's closer proximity to the Earth makes its influence on the tides roughly twice as strong as the Sun's. The Earth's rotation under these bulges of water results in two high waters and two low waters in a little over a day, known as the semidiurnal pattern. The Sun adds a secondary, weaker cycle to this pattern.

The terms "spring tides" and "neap tides" are mentioned, with spring tides occurring when the Sun and Moon are aligned and the difference between high and low water is at its maximum, while neap tides occur when the Sun and Moon are at right angles, and the difference between high and low water is at its minimum. The tidal range, or the vertical distance between high water and low water, varies greatly from place to place.

The Bay of Fundy, located on the eastern edge of North America, is noted for having some of the largest tidal ranges on Earth, exceeding fifteen meters during the strongest spring tides. The great range of the Bay of Fundy is attributed to its basin shape and the natural sloshing period of the water, which matches the semidiurnal tide rhythm.

The text also mentions variations in tidal patterns, including diurnal and mixed tides, which depend on the tilt of the Moon's orbit relative to the equator and the geography of each ocean basin. The mathematics used to describe these patterns break the tide into simple oscillations with different periods based on the Moon and Sun's motions.

Lastly, the horizontal movement of water accompanying the rise and fall of tides is referred to as tidal current, and its impact on coastal ecology is discussed. The regular flushing provided by these currents shapes the life in estuaries and mudflats, adapting communities to the daily cycle of submergence and exposure.
