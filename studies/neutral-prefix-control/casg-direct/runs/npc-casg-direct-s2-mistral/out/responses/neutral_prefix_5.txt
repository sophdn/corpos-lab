To summarize the information about tides, the essential cause of a tide is the difference in the gravitational pull of the Moon across the width of the Earth, which creates two bulges, one facing the Moon and one opposite it. As the Earth rotates beneath these bulges, a given stretch of coastline passes through regions of raised and lowered water, resulting in two high waters and two low waters in a little over a day. The Sun adds a secondary, weaker cycle to the lunar cycle, and the tidal range varies greatly from place to place. The Bay of Fundy has one of the largest tidal ranges on Earth due to its geography and the natural sloshing period of the basin. Not all coastlines follow the twice-daily rhythm; some experience a single high water and a single low water each day, a pattern called diurnal. The horizontal movement of water during the rise and fall is known as the tidal current.
