 In response to the passage about tides, the explanation provided details how the gravitational forces of the Earth, Moon, and Sun cause the tides. The Moon's proximity to Earth results in a stronger gravitational pull on the ocean, which dominates the lunar rhythm across most of the world's coastlines. The Earth's rotation beneath these bulges results in two high waters and two low waters, a rhythm known as the semidiurnal pattern.

The Sun also influences the tides with a weaker cycle. During new and full moons, the solar pull reinforces the lunar pull, causing spring tides with larger differences between high and low water. Conversely, during quarter phases, the Sun and Moon's pulls cancel partially, resulting in neap tides with smaller differences between high and low water.

The vertical distance between high water and low water is called the tidal range, which varies greatly from place to place. The Bay of Fundy in North America has one of the largest ranges due to its geography and the basin's natural sloshing period closely matching the tide's semidiurnal rhythm.
