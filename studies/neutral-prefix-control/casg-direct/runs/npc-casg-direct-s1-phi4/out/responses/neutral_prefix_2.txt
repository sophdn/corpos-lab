The passage describes the phenomenon of ocean tides, which are primarily caused by the gravitational interactions between the Earth, Moon, and Sun. Here's a breakdown of the key points:

1. **Gravitational Influence**: The tides are not caused by the gravitational pull of the Moon or Sun alone but by the differential gravitational forces they exert on different parts of the Earth. The Moon, despite being smaller than the Sun, exerts a stronger gravitational influence on Earth's oceans because it is much closer.

2. **Ocean Bulges**: This differential pull creates two tidal bulges on Earth—one facing the Moon and one on the opposite side. As Earth rotates, these bulges cause high and low tides at various points along coastlines.

3. **Tidal Patterns**:
   - **Semidiurnal Tides**: Most coastlines experience two high and two low tides each day due to the Earth's rotation under these tidal bulges.
   - **Spring and Neap Tides**: The alignment of the Sun and Moon affects tidal ranges. Spring tides occur during new and full moons when the Sun and Moon's gravitational pulls align, resulting in higher high tides and lower low tides. Neap tides occur during quarter moons when the Sun and Moon are at right angles, causing less extreme tides.

4. **Tidal Range Variability**: The vertical difference between high and low tides, known as the tidal range, varies significantly. Factors influencing this include the shape of the ocean basin and natural resonance. The Bay of Fundy in North America is noted for its extreme tidal ranges due to its natural sloshing period matching the tidal period.

5. **Tidal Patterns Variations**: Not all coastlines follow the semidiurnal pattern. Some experience a diurnal pattern (one high and one low tide per day), while others have mixed tides (two high or low tides of differing heights each day). These variations are influenced by the Moon's orbit tilt and the geography of ocean basins.

6. **Tidal Currents**: The horizontal movement of water associated with tides is called tidal currents. These include flood currents (inward flow during rising tides) and ebb currents (outward flow during falling tides), with slack water occurring between them. Tidal currents significantly impact marine ecosystems, especially in estuaries and mudflats.
