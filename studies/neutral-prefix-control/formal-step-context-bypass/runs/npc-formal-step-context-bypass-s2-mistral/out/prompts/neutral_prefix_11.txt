[INST] The tides of the ocean arise from the gravitational attraction between the Earth, the Moon, and the Sun. Every mass in the solar system pulls on every other mass, and the strength of that pull falls off with the square of the distance between them. Because the ocean is a fluid layer spread across a rotating planet, it responds to these pulls by shifting its shape over the course of each day. The Moon, though far smaller than the Sun, sits close enough to the Earth that its gravitational influence on the ocean is roughly twice as strong as the Sun's. For this reason the lunar rhythm dominates the pattern of the tides across most of the world's coastlines. The essential cause of a tide is not the raw pull of the Moon but the difference in that pull across the width of the Earth. The side of the planet nearest the Moon feels a slightly stronger attraction than the planet's center, while the far side feels a slightly weaker one. This gradient stretches the ocean into two bulges, one facing the Moon and one directly opposite it. As the Earth rotates beneath these bulges, a given stretch of coastline passes through regions of raised and lowered water. Most shorelines therefore experience two high waters and two low waters in a little over a day, a rhythm known as the semidiurnal pattern. The Sun adds a second, weaker cycle layered on top of the lunar one. When the Sun, the Earth, and the Moon fall into a line, near the times of the new moon and the full moon, the solar pull reinforces the lunar pull. The two bulges grow, and the difference between high and low water reaches its widest. Mariners call these the spring tides, a name that has nothing to do with the season and everything to do with the water springing upward. When the Sun and the Moon stand at right angles to each other, near the quarter phases, their pulls partly cancel. The difference between high and low water then shrinks to its smallest, and these are the neap tides. The vertical distance between high water and low water is called the tidal range, and it varies enormously from place to place. In the open ocean, far from any coast, the range may be little more than half a meter. Near certain coastlines the range grows to many meters. The Bay of Fundy on the eastern edge of North America records some of the largest ranges on Earth, exceeding fifteen meters during the strongest spring tides. Such extremes owe less to the Moon directly than to the shape of the basin that holds the water. A long, narrowing inlet can funnel an incoming tide into an ever smaller channel, piling the water higher as it advances. An even more striking amplification occurs through resonance. Every enclosed or partly enclosed body of water has a natural period at which it prefers to slosh back and forth, much as water in a bathtub rocks from end to end when disturbed. If that natural period happens to match the roughly twelve-hour rhythm of the semidiurnal tide, the two reinforce each other, and the tidal range within the basin swells far beyond what the astronomical forces alone would produce. The great range of the Bay of Fundy arises largely because the basin's natural sloshing period lies close to the period of the tide that drives it. Not all coastlines follow the twice-daily rhythm. In some parts of the world a single high water and a single low water occur each day, a pattern called diurnal. In others the two daily highs differ markedly in height, one running well above the other, in what is termed a mixed tide. These variations trace back to the tilt of the Moon's orbit relative to the equator and to the particular geography of each ocean basin, which channels and reflects the tidal waves in its own way. The mathematics that describes these patterns breaks the tide into a sum of simple oscillations, each with its own period drawn from the motions of the Moon and the Sun. Coastal authorities combine dozens of these components to forecast the height of the water far into the future. The horizontal movement of water that accompanies the rise and fall is known as the tidal current. As the tide rises, water flows inward in what is called the flood; as it falls, the water drains outward in the ebb. Between the two, at the turning of the tide, comes a brief interval of near stillness called slack water. In narrow straits and the mouths of rivers these currents can run swiftly, carrying sediment, nutrients, and the larvae of countless marine creatures with them. The regular flushing they provide shapes the ecology of estuaries and mudflats, sustaining communities of life adapted to the daily cycle of submergence and exposure. Tides also act, very slowly, upon the motions that create them. The friction of the moving water against the shallow floors of the seas drains a small amount of energy from the Earth's rotation. Over long ages this friction lengthens the day by a tiny fraction of a second each century, and it nudges the Moon gradually outward into a wider orbit. The rhythm we observe along any shoreline is thus a passing arrangement, a snapshot of a system that has been changing for billions of years and will go on changing long after the present coastlines have been reshaped by the same patient forces. Human understanding of the tides grew slowly across many centuries. Ancient observers noticed the link between the Moon and the water, but a full account had to wait for a theory of gravity that could explain why a distant body would move the seas at all.
