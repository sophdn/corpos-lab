* The tides are caused by the gravitational pull of the Moon and the Sun on the Earth's oceans, with the Moon's influence being stronger due to its proximity. The Moon's gravitational pull creates two bulges in the ocean, one facing the Moon and one opposite it. As the Earth rotates, these bulges cause two high waters and two low waters in a little over a day, a rhythm known as the semidiurnal pattern. The Sun adds a weaker cycle on top of the lunar one, and the difference between high and low water widens during spring tides (when the Sun and Moon are aligned) and shrinks during neap tides (when they are at right angles). The tidal range (vertical distance between high and low water) varies greatly from place to place, with the Bay of Fundy having some of the largest ranges on Earth. The tidal range can be amplified by the shape of the basin holding the water and by resonance with the natural period of sloshing within the basin.
* In some parts of the world, a single high water and a single low water occur each day (diurnal tides), while in others the two daily highs differ markedly (mixed tides). These variations are due to the tilt of the Moon's orbit and the particular geography of each ocean basin.
