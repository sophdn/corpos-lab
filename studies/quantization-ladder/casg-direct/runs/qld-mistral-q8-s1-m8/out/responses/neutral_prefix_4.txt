 In this response, I will discuss the relationship between tides and the gravitational forces exerted by the Moon and the Sun, and then provide a summary of the recent release of the 'filterpipe' project.

Tides are caused by the gravitational pull of the Moon and Sun on Earth's oceans. The Moon has a stronger influence on tides because it is closer to the Earth. This gravitational pull creates a difference in the attraction across the width of the Earth, stretching the ocean into two bulges, one facing the Moon and one directly opposite it. As the Earth rotates beneath these bulges, coastal areas experience two high waters and two low waters in a little over a day, known as the semidiurnal pattern. The Sun adds a second, weaker cycle to the tidal pattern, and the difference between high and low water changes depending on the alignment of the Sun, Earth, and Moon. When they align, high water reaches its widest, and these are known as spring tides. When they are at right angles, the difference between high and low water shrinks, and these are neap tides.

The tidal range (vertical distance between high water and low water) varies greatly from place to place, and some coastlines experience larger ranges due to their geography. For example, the Bay of Fundy in North America has one of the largest tidal ranges on Earth, exceeding fifteen meters during strong spring tides. This is partly due to the shape of the basin, which funnels and amplifies the incoming tide. Resonance between the natural sloshing period of the basin and the tidal rhythm can also cause an amplification of the tidal range.
