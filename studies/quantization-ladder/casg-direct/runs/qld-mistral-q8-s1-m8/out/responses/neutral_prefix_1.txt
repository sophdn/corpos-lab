1. The tides are caused by the gravitational pull of the Moon and the Sun on the Earth's oceans. The Moon's pull is stronger because it is closer to the Earth.

2. The difference in the Moon's pull across the width of the Earth causes two bulges in the ocean, one facing the Moon and one directly opposite it. As the Earth rotates, these bulges cause high and low waters on the coastlines.

3. Most shorelines experience two high waters and two low waters in a little over a day, a rhythm known as the semidiurnal pattern.

4. The Sun also influences the tides, adding a second, weaker cycle. When the Sun, the Earth, and the Moon are aligned (new moon or full moon), the solar pull reinforces the lunar pull, causing spring tides with larger differences between high and low water. When the Sun and Moon are at right angles (quarter phases), their pulls partly cancel, causing neap tides with smaller differences.

5. The tidal range, or the vertical distance between high water and low water, varies greatly from place to place. In the open ocean, it may be less than half a meter, while in certain coastlines, it can exceed fifteen meters.

6. The shape of the basin and resonance can greatly affect the tidal range. A long, narrowing inlet can funnel an incoming tide, and an enclosed or partly enclosed body of water can resonate with the tidal rhythm, amplifying the tidal range.

7. The horizontal movement of water that accompanies the rise and fall is known as the tidal current. This current can run swiftly and carry sediment, nutrients, and marine larvae.

8. Coastal authorities use complex mathematics to forecast the height of the water and the tidal current far into the future.
