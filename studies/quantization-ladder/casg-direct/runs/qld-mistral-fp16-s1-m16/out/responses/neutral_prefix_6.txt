Ocean tides are primarily caused by the gravitational interactions between the Earth, Moon, and Sun. The Moon's proximity to the Earth results in a stronger gravitational influence on the ocean, leading to two bulges - one facing the Moon and one opposite it. As the Earth rotates beneath these bulges, regions of raised and lowered water pass over coastal areas, resulting in two high waters and two low waters in a little over a day, known as the semidiurnal pattern. The Sun adds a weaker cycle to this pattern, and the combined effect leads to spring tides (large tidal ranges) during the new moon and full moon, and neap tides (small tidal ranges) during the quarter phases. The tidal range varies greatly from place to place, with some regions experiencing over fifteen meters during spring tides. The shape of the basin holding the water, as well as the natural sloshing period of the basin, can significantly affect tidal ranges.
